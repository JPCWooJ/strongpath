## Why these five, and not others

Most brand guides online are marketing-agency brochures for other marketing agencies. The five below are the opposite — they are the operating documents of organizations that publish at scale and cannot afford to sound stupid or get sued. Each one teaches StrongPath something distinct:

## 1. The voice/tone architecture (from Mailchimp)

**The most useful idea on the internet for brand consistency.**

Voice is constant. Tone shifts. Think of yourself: you have the same voice at a dinner with friends and in a meeting with your boss — but a dramatically different tone. A brand works the same way.

**StrongPath application:**
- **Voice (constant):** Serious. Direct. Hopeful. (Section 2 of BRAND.md)
- **Tone (shifts by audience):** Peer-level for Margaret. Reassuring and directive for David. Professional, peer-to-peer for Dr. Chen. (Section 4 of BRAND.md)

Mailchimp's full discipline is more elaborate than this — they distinguish *voice*, *tone*, *register*, and *style* — but for a brand our size, the voice/tone split is the only one that matters operationally. The other distinctions are useful at scale.

The most quotable rule from Mailchimp: *"We're not chefs. We're chef instructors."* The voice is constant; the recipe shifts. They are teaching, not performing. This is exactly the StrongPath posture — we are explaining the research, not performing wellness.

### One Mailchimp lesson StrongPath can ignore

Mailchimp emphasizes humor as a core voice element. We don't. Humor is fine in moderation, but the StrongPath brand voice is not humor-led. The reader is making a serious decision about their independence at age 70 or their parent's recovery from a fall. Our humor is the dry, sparing kind that an unintimidated specialist might use — never the punchy startup wit that makes Mailchimp's voice work.

## 2. Plain language as an ethos, not a rule (from GOV.UK)

**The single most counter-intuitive insight in good content writing.**

GOV.UK's research finding: *"Higher-literacy readers actually prefer plain English."* This contradicts most amateur writers' instinct, which is to elevate the diction when writing for educated audiences. The instinct is wrong.

The reason: educated readers scan. They're time-constrained. Plain English communicates faster and respects their time. Elevated diction flags effort over substance and reads as performative — which is the failure mode of brands that mistake "audience over 50 with graduate degrees" for "audience that wants to be talked up to."

### What this means for StrongPath

Margaret has a graduate degree. She reads *The Atlantic*. She does not want copy that sounds like *The Atlantic* trying to impress her. She wants copy that sounds like a well-regarded specialist explaining the situation directly.

GOV.UK's rule applies: write in the simplest language that does not sacrifice precision. Use technical terms when they're load-bearing (sarcopenia, dynapenia, motor unit denervation, progressive resistance). Strip every term that isn't.

### The "words to avoid" discipline

GOV.UK maintains a public list of words that fail their plain-language test. These overlap heavily with our own forbidden vocabulary in BRAND.md section 3.3, but a few are worth highlighting because they sneak past most reviewers:

- *"Robust."* Vague intensifier that adds nothing concrete. *"Robust evidence"* should always become *"strong evidence"* or *"meta-analyses since 1994 have shown."*
- *"Drive,"* *"deliver,"* *"leverage,"* *"empower."* Corporate verbs that signal effort, not action. Cut them and the sentence usually improves.
- *"Streamline,"* *"facilitate,"* *"utilize."* Always replaceable. *"Use"* beats *"utilize"* every time.
- *"Foster,"* *"foster a sense of."* Especially common in caregiver-adjacent copy. Cut.
- *"Holistic."* Wellness-brand vocabulary. Use *"comprehensive"* if the meaning is real; cut otherwise.

### A concrete StrongPath rewrite

**Before (corporate voice):** *"StrongPath leverages cutting-edge research on sarcopenia to empower adults to take a holistic approach to robust musculoskeletal health throughout their aging journey."*

**After (StrongPath voice):** *"StrongPath teaches adults what the muscle-loss research actually says — and what to do about it."*

The first version is 28 words and says nothing. The second is 17 words and says the thing.

## 3. Medical-adjacent content: how Harvard does it (from Harvard Health Publishing)

**The closest direct competitor in method, even though we don't share scale.**

HHP is the consumer health education arm of Harvard Medical School. Every article is reviewed by an HMS faculty physician. They publish nearly 2,000 articles. They take no outside advertising (subscription-funded, like the editorial model we should aspire to). Their stated goal: *"clinically accurate content in easy-to-understand language."*

### What HHP does that we should emulate

**1. Medical review is structural, not decorative.**
Every HHP article names the MD who reviewed it — credentials listed, on the page, visible. The structural pattern is real, and StrongPath should adopt it whenever the brand can. In Phase 1, no medical advisor is on the brand and the equivalent role is not filled — per BRAND.md §5, citing a reviewer the brand does not have would be a credibility failure worse than not having one. The Phase 1 substitute is citation discipline: every substantive claim is anchored in a current, named third-party source — peer-reviewed research, Harvard Health Publishing, Mayo Clinic, NIH — visible on the page, with the author, year, and journal where applicable. Citation discipline is what the brand is reviewed against until a credentialed advisor joins in Phase 2 (BRAND.md §5 Authority rules). At that point, the HHP-style named-reviewer pattern becomes available and the brand should adopt it.

**2. Conservative interpretation, common-sense risk framing.**
HHP is famous for *not* leaning into health hype. When a new study comes out, HHP explains what it found, what it didn't find, what its limitations were, and what the reader should (and should not) conclude. They underpromise. This is exactly our tonal reference — the opposite of the influencer/supplement space.

From a consumer review of HHP: *"Evidence-based summaries: articles and newsletters that synthesize clinical guidelines, meta-analyses, and reputable studies into plain-language recommendations. Emphasis on conservative interpretation and common-sense risk framing."*

Rewrite as a StrongPath rule: **when in doubt, underpromise.** A brand that underpromises and cites its sources earns the reader. A brand that overpromises gets clicks and loses trust.

**3. No external advertising on editorial content.**
HHP does not run banner ads. Revenue is subscriptions and licensed content. Per BP-07 and the broader brand, StrongPath should adopt the same posture: no display ads, no affiliate-spam content. Affiliate revenue (Amazon book links, eventually supplement links) is acceptable; banner ads for unrelated products are not. They destroy the editorial signal that the brand depends on.

**4. Plain-language titles that telegraph the answer.**
Scan HHP's recent article titles: *"Need more energy? Do the very thing you don't feel you have enough energy to do: exercise." "Gaining or just maintaining muscle mass becomes harder after middle age."* Titles lead with the finding, not the hook. This is the exact editorial pattern for StrongPath article titles. We are not writing *The Shocking Truth About Muscle Loss*. We are writing *After 50, strength training matters more than cardio — here's why.*

### What HHP does that we should *not* emulate

**1. Their tonal register is slightly more institutional than ours should be.** HHP is writing from the voice of Harvard Medical School. We are writing from the voice of a founder and operator who took the baton from the book's authors and is continuing the path in the form 2026 calls for. We can be warmer, more personal, and more first-person than HHP — Jeff is a founder, not a faculty member. HHP sets the floor for credibility; we extend above it with a more human voice (per BRAND.md §5 founder narrative).

**2. Their newsletter conversion patterns lean old-school.** HHP sells through long-form landing pages for their subscription newsletters (*Harvard Health Letter*, *Harvard Women's Health Watch*, etc.) with heavy offer copy. We are building a different monetization structure — affiliate, digital programs, membership, eventually supplements. We don't need to copy their sales patterns.

## 4. The Economist's editorial discipline

**Our tonal reference for what authoritative-but-accessible sounds like.**

From the guide itself: *"Clarity of writing usually follows clarity of thought."* This one line is the entire thesis. If your writing is muddled, the thinking behind it is probably muddled. The fix isn't better editing — it's better thinking. This is the discipline we're after.

### The three Economist disciplines that apply to StrongPath

**1. Active voice, almost always.**
From every source, but the Economist holds it most strictly. Active voice names the actor and the action; passive voice hides them. For medical-adjacent writing, this matters more than elsewhere — passive voice is what makes health copy sound evasive and lawyered. *"Muscle is lost with age"* is weak and vaguely fatalistic. *"Adults over 50 lose 1–2% of their muscle mass per year unless they train against resistance"* is specific, active, and hopeful — because it names the mechanism of prevention.

**2. Short sentences for weight; long sentences for nuance; never long sentences to sound smart.**
The Economist's writers alternate deliberately. A page of only-short sentences reads aggressive. A page of only-long sentences reads academic. The mix is the craft. For StrongPath: most paragraphs should open with a short sentence that makes a claim, then a longer sentence that qualifies it, then a short sentence that summarizes. This is the editorial cadence we want.

**3. Every word earns its place.**
The Economist's maxim: *"Every word should contribute to the overall meaning. Unnecessary words, phrases, and clauses should be ruthlessly eliminated."*

Specifically ruthless about:
- Redundancies — *"completely finished" → finished. "absolutely essential" → essential. "end result" → result.*
- Throat-clearing openers — *"It is important to note that..."* → delete the first seven words.
- Hedging pile-ups — *"may potentially possibly help"* → "may help."
- Nominalizations — *"conducted an investigation of"* → "investigated."

### One Economist rule StrongPath does not follow

**The Economist avoids contractions.** We do not. The Economist is a global print weekly writing for a particular register; we are a digital brand writing to Americans aged 45-75 who want to feel addressed as humans. Contractions are part of what makes prose feel like a person talking. *"We're building a serious platform"* lands better than *"We are building a serious platform"* for most of our copy. On formal deliverables — the About page, the medical disclaimer, a press release — we use full forms. Everywhere else, contractions are fine.

## 5. The synthesized rules — what actually goes into every StrongPath draft

Collapsing the above into rules a writer or AI agent can actually apply. These extend BRAND.md section 3 (voice rules) with patterns proven at scale by the above publishers.

### Pre-writing checklist (15 seconds before you start)

1. Name the primary reader (which persona)
2. Name their emotional state at the moment they would land on this page (curious? overwhelmed? skeptical?)
3. Name the single action they should take after reading
4. Name the one sentence that would make them take it

If any of these four are vague, the writing will be vague.

### Sentence-level rules

1. **Lead with the finding, not the hook.** *"After 50, strength matters more than cardio"* beats *"What I'm about to tell you changed everything."*
2. **Active voice unless the passive actively helps.** Passive is legitimate when the object matters more than the actor ("The study was retracted"), rare otherwise.
3. **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence has two commas and a "however," split it.
4. **Concrete nouns, strong verbs.** Avoid "things," "aspects," "elements," "factors" as placeholder nouns. Name what you mean.
5. **No throat-clearing.** "It is important to note that" / "In terms of" / "With respect to" — cut.
6. **No filler intensifiers.** "very," "really," "quite," "literally" — cut, or replace the adjective with a stronger one.
7. **Contractions are welcome in body copy.** Full forms on formal deliverables.
8. **Numbers: use the specific number.** "Adults lose 15% of muscle mass between 50 and 70" beats "significant muscle loss with age."
9. **Citations in-line when they strengthen the point.** "The 2019 meta-analysis in *JAMA*" beats "studies show."
10. **Every paragraph should earn its space.** If a paragraph can be cut without loss, cut it.

### Paragraph-level rules

1. **Open with the claim.** A 65-year-old skimming on a phone reads the first sentence of each paragraph. Make it carry the paragraph.
2. **Short paragraphs for weight and readability.** Two to four sentences is the default. Six-sentence paragraphs only when the argument needs the length.
3. **Vary paragraph length deliberately.** Short paragraph, short paragraph, medium paragraph, short paragraph. The rhythm keeps the reader in the text.

### Page-level rules

1. **One primary action per page.** Surface it above the fold. Secondary actions go lower.
2. **Descriptive headings, not clever ones.** *"How to train after 60"* beats *"The Strong Path Forward"* for SEO, accessibility, and reader scan patterns.
3. **Frontload keywords.** In headings, in link text, in the first sentence. Readers scan by the first two or three words of each block.
4. **Every substantive-claim page shows the book cover and cites at least one named authority.** Non-negotiable per BRAND.md section 5.

### Things we will not do, regardless of conversion pressure

1. No fabricated testimonials or member counts (BP-07)
2. No "miracle," "secret," "hack," "reverse," "cure" language (BRAND.md section 3)
3. No product claims dressed as book claims (BRAND.md section 9)
4. No paid-influencer endorsements without FTC-compliant disclosure
5. No dark-pattern urgency timers or fake scarcity ("Only 3 left! Expires in 4:58!")
6. No AI-generated content published without human review
7. No pop-ups that block the content on first visit (fast way to burn trust with a 60+ reader)

## 6. How this file is used going forward

**During drafting** (any workstream — content, email, paid media): reference the sentence-level and paragraph-level rules above alongside BRAND.md section 3. Write the draft against these rules, not against them.

**During review** (Brand Ambassador's primary function): the rubric in BRAND.md section 10 is the primary checklist. This file is the explanation of *why* each rubric item exists. When a writer pushes back on a rubric decision, this file has the answer in more detail.

**During onboarding** (new workstream chat, new team member, new agent): read BRAND.md first, this file second. BRAND.md is binding. This file is reference.

**When to update this file:** when a new credible reference comes into view (not every marketing blog — actual working style guides from serious publishers), distill the net-new principles and add a section. Do not re-summarize guides that teach the same things we already have.

## 7. What's not in this file and why

I deliberately did not include:

- **The NYT, WSJ, or AP style guides.** These are useful for copy editors but do not teach voice or brand. They answer "serial comma or not" — not "how should a health brand sound."
- **Nielsen Norman Group UX writing research.** High-quality and influential, but mostly covers microcopy (buttons, form labels, error messages) that we can handle with default good practices. Revisit when we're designing the AI advisor and the quiz interface.
- **Brand guides from consumer health competitors** (Peloton, Oura, Whoop, Noom). These are marketing-led and skew in directions we don't want (hype, aspirational imagery, influencer voice). Reading them is fine as intel; adopting their patterns would misposition us.
- **Academic writing guides** (APA, AMA manuals). These are for researchers writing for researchers. We're translating research, not producing it.

If any of these become relevant — for example, if we launch the AI advisor and need stronger UX writing discipline — I'll pull the applicable guide then and extend this file.
