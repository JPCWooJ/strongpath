# Persona 1 — The Pre-Frail Adult

## "Margaret" — 62, recently noticed the stairs

*Co-primary persona. Phase 1 priority.*

### 1. Identity

Margaret is 62 years old. She's noticed something. Not a diagnosis, not a crisis — a quiet accumulation. The stairs to her second-floor bedroom feel longer than they used to. She adjusted how she carries groceries from the car without realizing she had. Her knee did something strange getting off a low couch at her daughter's house, and she laughed it off in the moment. Later, she was not laughing.

She is not frail. She is not declining. She is paying attention. And she is beginning to suspect that the advice she grew up on — walk 30 minutes a day, eat some fruits and vegetables — is not going to be enough for the next 25 years.

### 2. Demographics

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Age** | 55–72, heavy concentration 58–68. Margaret is 62 because that's the moment of maximum signal: recently retired or considering it, financially stable, health still largely intact, first clear physical signals landing. |
| **Gender** | Roughly 55–60% female. Women live longer, face sarcopenia outcomes earlier relative to life expectancy, and are more likely to research health topics online. Men are meaningful but smaller in the top of the funnel — they convert later in the funnel when the proof is undeniable. |
| **Income** | Household $75K–$250K+. Not a mass-market persona. The book and the brand signal "serious," which means our audience self-selects toward educated professional households. |
| **Education** | Four-year degree minimum; 40%+ hold graduate degrees. Many are or were professionals — attorneys, doctors, educators, executives, small-business owners. |
| **Location** | Suburban and urban; disproportionately in markets with strong educational and professional populations (Boston, DC metro, Chicago North Shore, SF Bay, Seattle, Austin, Denver, the Florida corridor from Naples to West Palm). |
| **Life stage** | Empty nest or near-empty nest. Adult children launched or launching. Parents aging, dying, or recently deceased. Often still working — frequently planning for an unretirement that looks different from their parents' retirement. |
| **Health baseline** | Broadly healthy. May manage one or two chronic conditions (hypertension, elevated cholesterol, mild osteoarthritis) well. No acute crisis. Current exercise habit: usually walking, sometimes yoga or swimming. Rarely strength training at meaningful intensity. |

### 3. Psychographics

Margaret is defined by one trait: **she refuses to accept the default.** This trait shows up across every domain of her life — in her career, her parenting, her politics, her investments. She has spent forty years noticing when the official story doesn't match the evidence and quietly acting on what she sees. When she faces a problem, her instinct is to research, then act.

She is not motivated by vanity. She does not want to "look 30 at 60." She does not want to be called "ageless" — she finds the word embarrassing. She wants to be **capable.** She wants to keep doing the things she does. She wants to travel with her husband. She wants to be the grandmother who actually plays on the floor. She wants to carry her own luggage. She wants, above all, the ability to stay in control of her own life for as long as possible.

She is also, quietly, afraid. Not of dying. She has mostly made peace with that. She is afraid of the thing she watched her mother go through — the gradual loss of independence, the move to assisted living, the call from a sibling about another fall. She is afraid of the humiliation of the decades between strong and gone. She does not say this out loud. It is the engine under everything.

She is skeptical of most health content on the internet. She has encountered enough supplement scams, fad diets, and transformation promises to have developed a filter. She trusts sources the way she trusts a well-regarded specialist — she wants to see credentials, citations, and a tone that doesn't try too hard.

*Note on v2. Margaret has not been reality-checked against a founder who sits inside this persona. The fear framing used here was derived from the book's rhetoric and known demographic research. Live customer data will sharpen this persona over time. If the caregiver reframe (love, not fear) has an analog in the Pre-Frail Adult, v3 may need to adjust this section.*

### 4. The Moment — Where She Is When She Finds Us

Most likely entry points:

- **The stairs moment.** She notices something physical — a stair that felt harder, a grocery bag that felt heavier, a low chair she struggled to get out of. She Googles it at 11pm. She doesn't want to overreact. She wants data.
- **The parent moment.** A parent is diagnosed with something age-related, or falls, or moves to assisted living. Margaret is suddenly staring at a possible future. She is not researching for her parent — she is researching for herself.
- **The friend moment.** A close friend or colleague breaks a hip, or gets a diagnosis, or loses independence suddenly. Margaret is 62; her peers are entering the zone where this starts happening. Each event is a data point.
- **The annual physical.** Her primary care doctor said "you should start lifting weights" and didn't give her anything specific. She went home and tried to figure out what that meant. The internet told her contradictory things. She wants a serious answer.

**Her emotional state at this moment.** Alert but not panicked. Intellectually curious. Mildly defensive — she does not want to be called old, frail, or at-risk, and will close any page that makes her feel those things. Ready to commit to something serious if the source is credible. Exhausted by content that treats her like she's 40 (boot camp) or 85 (chair yoga).

### 6. Where She Lives

**Where she spends time:**

- Reads *The Atlantic*, *The New York Times*, *The New Yorker*, often *The Wall Street Journal*
- Listens to *The Daily*, *Fresh Air*, *The Ezra Klein Show*, *Hidden Brain*, sometimes *Huberman Lab* (skeptically — the hype grates but the science sometimes lands)
- Uses Facebook for family, LinkedIn for professional, increasingly Substack for writers she trusts
- Watches PBS, BBC, sometimes premium streaming documentaries
- Member of at least one of: a book club, a local hiking group, a church or synagogue, a professional alumni network, a museum or symphony

**Where she searches:**

- Google for information, increasingly with follow-ups to AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude — she started using these in the last 18 months)
- Amazon for books — this is where she first encounters *Choosing the StrongPath* or ones like it
- Harvard Health Publishing, Mayo Clinic, NIH/NIA for health information she considers reliable
- Reddit sometimes for "what are people actually doing" (r/AskOldPeople, r/Longevity, r/xxfitness)

**Who she trusts:**

- Her primary care physician — but with limits; she has caught him missing things
- Specialists she's been referred to — the hierarchy of expertise matters
- A few well-regarded health writers (NYT's Jane Brody alumni; specific authors of books she's read cover to cover)
- Her peer group, filtered — the friend who started lifting at 55 and still skis is more persuasive than any ad

### 7. Objections — Why She Doesn't Convert on First Visit

1. **"This looks like every other wellness site."** If the visual register is supplement-brand aesthetic — stock photos of grey-haired couples on beaches, gold-heavy palette, exclamation points, "unlock your best self" — she bounces in under 10 seconds. BRAND.md's editorial direction exists precisely because of this bounce.
2. **"I don't need someone telling me to walk 10,000 steps."** She has read the basics. Content that rehashes "eat your vegetables, get your steps, sleep 8 hours" signals we have nothing new to offer.
3. **"Is this a supplement pitch?"** If the first meaningful CTA is buying something, she distrusts the rest of the content retroactively. She wants the information to be the product at first touch.
4. **"Who wrote this?"** If she cannot find a named author, credentials, or citations within 30 seconds, the site fails her smell test. Anonymous health content is a red flag.
5. **"I'm not that person yet."** If the imagery or copy suggests she's being addressed as frail or declining, she rejects the framing. She sees herself as someone paying attention, not someone at risk.
6. **"This is going to be hard to do alone."** Even if the content is credible, she worries about executing it. Doing strength training badly is worse than not doing it at all — injury risk, no results, wasted effort.
7. **"My doctor didn't mention this."** If her physician has not flagged sarcopenia as important, part of her wonders if we are overstating it. This is actually our opportunity — the book itself addresses this head-on (Chapter 5: *Why Most Doctors Do Not Understand Sarcopenia*).

### 8. What Earns Her Trust

In order of potency:

1. **The book.** A physical book, cover visible, with an Amazon bestseller framing. A book signals the work has been done. A website alone does not.
2. **Named sources.** Cited researchers with institutions (Tufts, Harvard, Illinois, the NIH) — or specific studies with year and journal. She does not verify them, but their presence changes everything.
3. **Underpromising tone.** "Most adults can safely begin with…" reads as credible. "Transform your strength in 30 days" reads as scam. She has a highly calibrated filter for the difference.
4. **Editorial visual register.** Serif headlines, generous whitespace, 18px body type, restrained palette. These are signals before any word is read. She will register them in the first three seconds.
5. **Plain language about the mechanism.** Motor unit denervation, protein synthesis, progressive resistance. Not jargon for its own sake — the specific vocabulary that signals the writer knows the field.
6. **Acknowledgment of what she has already heard.** "You have probably been told to walk more. Walking is valuable, but it does not address strength." This is worth more than any testimonial.
7. **A credible path, not a miracle.** She wants to see the protocol — what exactly, how often, what progression. Specifics beat promises every time.

### 9. Messages That Land

**Openers that work for her:**

> "You have probably noticed your body behaving differently. You are not imagining it. Between ages 50 and 70, adults lose an average of 15% of their muscle mass — and more than half of their strength. This is measurable, predictable, and with the right protocol, largely preventable."

> "Sarcopenia is not a disease of aging. It is a disease of disuse. The research has been clear on this since 1995. The challenge has always been translating it into something you can actually do."

> "The instinct most physicians give — 'try to lift some weights' — is correct but insufficient. Here is what the research actually prescribes."

> "At 62, you have between 15 and 30 good years left. How those years go depends more on what you do in the next decade than on anything your doctor writes in a chart."

**Headlines that work for her:**

- "What the muscle-loss research actually says — and what your doctor probably didn't tell you"
- "Strength training after 60: the protocol, not the pep talk"
- "Why walking isn't enough, and what is"
- "The single measure that predicts the next 20 years of your health"

### 10. Messages That Fail

**Openers that do not work for her:**

- *"Aging doesn't have to mean slowing down!"* (exclamation point, generic, performative)
- *"Unlock your best body at any age"* (unlock, best body, both disqualifying)
- *"The shocking truth about muscle loss doctors don't want you to know"* (secret, conspiracy, fear)
- *"Let's face it — aging is hard."* (filler phrase, talking down)
- *"Transform your body in just 30 days!"* (transformation, timeline promise, exclamation)

**Visual treatments that fail her:**

- Stock photography of a silver-haired couple laughing on a beach
- A woman doing yoga on a dock at sunset
- Any "transformation" before/after
- Gold-dominant layouts (reads as Masonic lodge, per BRAND.md)
- Any pharmaceutical-ad aesthetic

# Persona 2 — The Proactive Caregiver

## "David" — 54, watching his father age and choosing to act

*Co-primary persona. Phase 1 priority. Distinct purchase psychology from the end user.*

*Founder reality check: Jeff Camp sits inside this persona. Caregiver-facing copy can be tested against his direct experience. This is a brand asset — use it.*

---

> ### Founder anchor for this persona
>
> *"If you could make your mother or father a little bit more comfortable just one day, you'll do it."*
>
> — Jeff Camp, April 2026
>
> This is the test. Every piece of David-facing copy, imagery, email, and ad is checked against this sentence. The caregiver acts out of love, not fear. If a draft rings false against this anchor, the draft is wrong. The review question is not "does this convert?" — it is "does this read as *eunoia* (Aristotle's term for goodwill) or does it read as fear?" Goodwill is the motivation we are serving, not one we are manufacturing.

---

### 1. Identity

David is 54. His father is 83. His mother is 81. Both still live largely independently, but David has been watching the slope for several years now — his father has lost a step, his mother has lost weight she did not need to lose, their pace has changed, their world has gotten smaller. Nothing is acute. Nothing is a crisis. But David is not naïve, and he can see where this is going if nothing changes.

David is not a caregiver in the professional sense. He has a job, a family, a marriage, a mortgage, two grown children, a dog. He is the person his family turns to because he is the kind of person who shows up — the son who flies in for the hospital stay, the sibling who talks to the doctors, the one who researches what comes next. He does this because he loves his parents. Not because he has to. Because he wants to.

His motivation is goodwill. He wants his parents to have good days. That is the entire engine of this persona. Not fear of the nursing home. Not grief at their decline. Not a projection of his own future. He simply loves them and wants them well. When he reads that strength training, protein, and recovery can measurably increase the number of good days his parents have left, he acts — because **if he could make his mother or father a little more comfortable just one day, he will.**

There is also, honestly, a second-order effect worth naming. David is 54. He has watched enough of his parents' generation to know what the alternative looks like. When he reads material written for his father and it lands on him too, he often starts training himself. This is not the dominant motivation — it is a byproduct of engaging with the StrongPath on behalf of someone he loves. But the byproduct is real, and the family-adherence dynamic it enables is one of the most important features of this persona.

### 2. Demographics

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Age** | 45–60, concentration 50–58. Peak caregiver age. Parents still independent but declining, or parents recently post-crisis. Children mostly launched. Still in career. |
| **Gender** | Roughly 55–60% female in caregiving responsibility overall, but male caregivers over-index when the parent is a father and when the adult child is analytically oriented. Protocol-based framing converts both genders; emotional-performance framing does not. |
| **Income** | Household $100K–$300K+. This persona has the means to purchase programs, books, and services for an aging parent without negotiation. Cost is rarely the dominant concern. |
| **Education** | Four-year degree strongly dominant. Often in a professional or managerial role — the same skill set that lets them run a project at work is the one they bring to their parents' care. |
| **Location** | Frequently located within a few hours of the aging parent; often in the same metro area. Disproportionately in urban and suburban areas with strong professional populations. |
| **Life stage** | Still working, often at career peak. Marriage usually intact. Children late teens to early thirties. "Sandwich generation" pressure is real — but the caregiving itself is not experienced as burden. It is experienced as love in action. |
| **Health baseline** | Their own health is often the least-optimized part of their life, not because they don't know better but because the hours go elsewhere. They know they should be training. They usually are not. This makes them excellent cross-over customers — they buy for the parent and, over time, join themselves. |

### 3. Psychographics

David is defined by **goodwill in action.** Aristotle's word for this is *eunoia* — goodwill, the wish for another's good for their own sake. It is the oldest and simplest motivation in caregiving, and it is the one that sustains the actual work once the easy part (giving someone a book) is over. Giving the book is easy. What comes after — encouraging, checking in, adapting, accepting that the parent may or may not follow through — that requires something more durable than fear or obligation.

David has a practical turn of mind. He is a problem-solver by disposition and often by profession. Given a problem, his instinct is to research it, understand the mechanism, and then act. But the problem he is solving here is not a *problem* in the way a work problem is. It has no clean solution. The direction of travel is one-way. What he is really doing is working to increase the number of good days his parents have — and accepting that this is the best available outcome.

He is quietly frustrated that most primary care physicians have little to offer on prevention. His parents' doctors have not mentioned sarcopenia. The advice they give is generic and insufficient. He does not carry this as a grievance against the healthcare system — he has too much goodwill and too realistic a view of what doctors can do in a 15-minute appointment. But the gap is there, and when he finds a resource that fills the gap credibly, he is relieved, not skeptical.

**A caregiver insight worth holding onto.** Caregiving is costly. It is time, money, emotional energy, and deferred attention to other parts of life. The founder puts it this way: *"It's the only debt I am happy to repay."* People pay mortgages without joy. They make car payments without joy. But they will move mountains to make a parent a little more comfortable — not because they are obligated to, but because they love. This sentence is the emotional center of the persona. Downstream copy should never frame caregiving as a burden. It isn't one.

### 4. The Moment — Three Moments, Not One

A critical v2 correction. v1 treated the caregiver's moment as a single event. In reality, there are three distinct moments — and only two of them are our audience. Confusing them will lead to wasted paid media and misaligned content.

#### Moment 1 — The Before

**Where:** The parent is aging, not in crisis. David is watching the slope and wants to do something preventive. He has bandwidth. He can read long-form. He can give his parent a book and have a real conversation about it. This is StrongPath's ideal entry point — the research lands before an event, the parent is still coachable, and the caregiver has emotional space to be patient with adherence.

**Emotional state:** Concerned but not panicked. Goodwill-motivated. Willing to invest time and money preventively. Open to long content.

**Search intent:** "How do I keep my parents strong," "what prevents falls," "strength training for 80-year-olds," "my mother is getting weaker," "is it too late for my father to start lifting."

**Priority:** High-value entry point. Content and SEO should be built to catch these queries.

#### Moment 2 — The Crisis

**Where:** An acute event has happened. A fall, a hospitalization, a surgery, a placement in skilled nursing. David is in navigation mode — where does my parent go, what does Medicare cover, how long is rehab, who provides home care, what is the discharge plan. He is not researching strength training. He is researching the healthcare system.

**Emotional state:** Reactive, task-focused, running on adrenaline and logistics. Not receptive to prevention content. Not our audience in this moment.

**Search intent:** "Skilled nursing facilities near me," "how long does hip replacement recovery take," "what does Medicare cover for rehab," "home health aide cost," "assisted living [city]."

**Priority:** Do not target. Paid media that bids on crisis-phase keywords burns money. SEO content that targets crisis queries competes with large, well-resourced systems (AARP, hospital discharge planning, state agencies) that are better positioned to answer them. StrongPath's place in the crisis moment is to not be in the way — we are waiting at the other end.

#### Moment 3 — The After

**Where:** The acute phase has stabilized. The parent is in a facility or back home. The immediate logistics are handled. David's attention returns to the longer-term question: how do I prevent this from happening again, and how do I help my parent rebuild? Prevention is now no longer abstract — the parent has just demonstrated exactly why strength matters.

**Emotional state:** Determined. Still tired. Newly persuaded of what preventive work could have saved them. Receptive to the book, to protocols, to programs, to anything credible.

**Search intent:** "Strength training after hip replacement," "how to prevent falls after a fall," "rehab at home for 80-year-old," "exercise after surgery for elderly," "can an 85-year-old get stronger."

**Priority:** Highest-intent entry point we have. The caregiver has just lived the cost of not having a preventive protocol in place. SEO, content, and paid media should heavily prioritize post-event queries. This is where the research lands hardest.

#### Across all three moments

The underlying emotional engine is constant: love, goodwill, the wish to make his parent a little more comfortable. The tactical framing of our messaging shifts by moment, but the core motivation does not. Never mistake the changing tactical surface for a changing emotional core.

### 7. Objections — Why He Doesn't Convert on First Visit

1. **"I've seen this before and it was a rehash."** He has purchased caregiver resources that turned out to be listicles. Our content has to be substantively different in the first three paragraphs.
2. **"My parent won't do it."** His biggest practical concern is adherence. He has a realistic view of his parent's willingness to follow a new protocol. We have to speak to that — what a program looks like for someone who is not naturally athletic, who has given up once or twice, who is embarrassed by their own weakness. This is also why the family-networked product concept (see `PRODUCT_CONCEPTS_BACKLOG.md`) maps so directly onto this persona.
3. **"Is this safe for them?"** He is careful about recommending anything that could hurt his parent. A program that starts too aggressive will be rejected; a program that starts too gentle will feel like theater. He needs to see that we understand the real safety considerations for an 80-year-old starting from a low baseline.
4. **"I don't have time to manage another thing."** His life is full. He cannot add a complex protocol to his load. He needs something that either runs itself (for his parent) or fits into his own life (for himself) without adding cognitive burden.
5. **"What does this actually cost, and what am I signing up for?"** He is a careful buyer. Surprise upsells, hidden subscriptions, and dark patterns will lose him permanently. Clear pricing, clear scope, clear path. Cost itself is rarely the blocker — opaque cost is the blocker.
6. **"Is this for him or for me?"** If the content is too focused on fit 60-year-olds, it doesn't feel like it applies to his 83-year-old father. If it is too focused on the very elderly and frail, it doesn't feel like it applies to him. We need both tracks visible on the same page.
7. **"Am I being sold fear?"** If the page leads with the nursing home as threat, or positions his parent's future as catastrophic, or performs emotional urgency — he disengages. Not because he doesn't care, but because he cares from a position of goodwill, not panic, and fear-based copy reads as manipulation.

### 8. What Earns His Trust

In order of potency:

1. **A credible path, not a pitch.** He wants to see the plan, then decide. A page that is all emotional framing and no substance will lose him in thirty seconds.
2. **The book.** Same power as for Margaret, for similar reasons. A book is a different class of credential than a website — and this book is, notably, written by men who have walked this path themselves.
3. **Acknowledgment that his parent's physician missed this — without blame.** The book itself names the gap in primary care education around sarcopenia. This validates his experience. Importantly, it names the gap without carrying a grievance — which is the correct register for this persona.
4. **Specificity about his parent's situation.** Content that says "for an 80-year-old starting from sedentary" is a thousand times more useful than content that says "for seniors." Specificity signals we have actually thought about his case.
5. **Warm, realistic framing of the parent.** His parent is not a victim and not a problem. Content that treats the parent with dignity — as someone who can still learn, adapt, and improve — earns trust in a way nothing else does.
6. **Real testimonials with outcomes and duration.** "My father went from not being able to stand from a chair to walking a mile within 4 months" is worth more than any claim. If we don't have this yet, we don't fake it (per BRAND.md section 9.4 and BP-07).
7. **A clinical-caliber Q&A section.** The specific questions he has — "is this safe for someone with osteoporosis," "what if he has had a knee replacement," "what about blood pressure medication" — answered with real clinical nuance and appropriate disclaimers.

### 9. Messages That Land

**Openers that work for him — goodwill-leading, not fear-leading:**

> "If you want your parents to have good days, strength is the most important variable you can still move. The research on this has been clear for thirty years. Most primary care physicians have not caught up with it yet."

> "You already know your parents' generation was not taught that strength matters in their 70s and 80s. It does. Here is what the research shows, and here is a program your parent can start this week — at whatever starting point they are at."

> "The best thing you can do for your parents right now is also one of the simplest. Not easy — simple. Resistance training, adequate protein, and recovery. The research is settled. What follows is the execution."

> "Most caregiver content is written for the caregiver alone. We write for the caregiver and the parent together. Share this with your father — the language was chosen with both of you in mind."

**Headlines that work for him:**

- "What the research actually says about keeping your parents strong"
- "Strength training for adults over 80 — yes, over 80. Here is the protocol."
- "Why your father's doctor didn't mention sarcopenia, and what you can do now"
- "Good days. That's what you can actually help them protect."
- "The program I wish I had found a year earlier" (for After-moment)

### 10. Messages That Fail

**Openers that do not work for him — the fear-leading versions cut from v1:**

- *"Don't let your father end up in a nursing home."* (fear-leading, threat-framed, reads as manipulation)
- *"Your parent is at risk — here's what you need to know."* (alarm-bell framing; the caregiver already knows their parent is aging, and scare-framing reads as disrespect to both of them)
- *"Being a caregiver is the hardest job there is. We understand."* (emotional performance; adds nothing; caregiving is hard, but the caregiver does not want it validated — they want a plan)
- *"You're not alone in this."* (he knows; he needs a protocol, not a support group)
- *"Here are 10 tips to help your aging loved one."* (listicle; he has read a hundred)
- *"Discover the secret to healthy aging!"* (secret, exclamation, generic)
- *"It's never too late to start!"* (pep-talk framing; "too late" is exactly the anxiety he does not want performed at him)

**Framings that fail him:**

- Anything that leads with the nursing home as threat
- Anything that positions caregiving as burden
- Anything that positions the caregiver as a victim of circumstances
- Heavy imagery of frail elderly people in hospital settings — feels hopeless
- Heavy imagery of fit 55-year-olds only — feels inapplicable to his father
- Long emotional essays about someone else's caregiving — he's reading for a plan, not for empathy
- Anything that frames his parent as "struggling" or "needing help" — his parent's self-respect is at stake and David is protective of it

### 11. Latent Product Demand

A structural product opportunity sits inside this persona that deserves separate documentation. The caregiver is willing to pay for adherence on behalf of the parent — and for himself. The parent is a user; the caregiver is a user; both are on the path together. A StrongPath product that solves for family-mediated accountability — visibility into each other's training, low-friction encouragement across generations, AI-augmented check-ins when a family member is unavailable — addresses a pain point no one in the family can solve alone. The adherence mechanism is multi-directional: the caregiver nudges the parent, the parent's progress motivates the caregiver, and both reinforce each other over time. This is the single highest-value feature gap in the current product concept.

*This concept is not a Phase 1 build. It is filed in `PRODUCT_CONCEPTS_BACKLOG.md` as Entry 1. It is referenced here because product demand is a persona characteristic, and downstream workstream chats should know the demand exists without trying to activate it prematurely.*

# Persona 3 — The Allied Health Professional

## "Dr. Chen" — 41, DPT, works at an outpatient clinic in a regional hospital

*Documented for completeness. **Phase 2 priority — not an active Phase 1 acquisition target.** See Phase 1 Scope below.*

### Phase 1 Scope — Why This Persona Is Not Active at Launch

The Allied Health Professional is a critical long-term channel. PTs, OTs, and geriatric nurses refer patients to resources they trust, and over time this persona drives high-quality, low-CAC traffic and high-trust conversions. However:

- **The conversion horizon is long.** A PT recommending StrongPath to a patient is a different sales cycle from a consumer Google search. It requires clinical collateral, continuing-education-caliber content, and relationships — none of which are in the Phase 1 budget.
- **The Phase 1 revenue targets do not require this persona.** Margaret and David drive the first $500–600 of affiliate revenue and the first 1,200 subscribers. Dr. Chen does not.
- **The wrong investment here is expensive.** Producing clinician-grade content requires credentialed review we don't have in Phase 1. Publishing half-credible clinical material damages the brand with this audience worse than not publishing at all.

**Phase 1 posture:** The `/for-clinicians` page exists and is plainly written. It does not run paid acquisition. Content is not optimized for clinical search terms. When clinicians find us organically, the page earns their respect — it does not try to convert them into a program.

**Phase 2 activation:** When Phase 1 revenue targets are hit and a medical advisor is in place, we build real clinical collateral — one-pagers, a referral kit, CEU-style content — and activate acquisition through professional channels. That is a separate work plan.

With that framing established, the persona is documented below for the Phase 2 team and for any Phase 1 asset that might be seen by this reader organically.

### 1. Identity

Dr. Chen is 41, a Doctor of Physical Therapy with nine years in practice. She works at a regional hospital's outpatient orthopedic and geriatric clinic, where she sees adults 60–90 for post-surgical rehab, balance and fall prevention, and general conditioning. She is good at her job. She knows the sarcopenia literature because she has to — it is the substrate of most of her caseload.

She is also frustrated. The primary-care physicians who refer to her frequently have no conceptual model of muscle loss as a preventable condition. She gets patients at the point of intervention, not prevention, and she sees the pattern repeat. She wants to hand her patients a resource they can engage with between visits — one she can trust not to damage her reputation.

### 2. Demographics

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Age** | 28–60. Dr. Chen is 41 because that's the experience peak — enough clinical judgment to evaluate, not so late-career that she's disengaged. |
| **Gender** | PT field is roughly 60% female; OT and geriatric nursing skew higher female. Our messaging will be slightly female-skewed as a result. |
| **Income** | $75K–$130K depending on role and region. Not our purchase target — she recommends, she does not buy for herself. |
| **Education** | Clinical doctorate (DPT), OTD, MSN, or RN+specialty certification. |
| **Location** | Everywhere. Geographically dispersed; where the patients are. |
| **Role** | Outpatient clinic, skilled nursing facility, home health, academic medical center, private practice. The highest-value segment for StrongPath is outpatient orthopedic and geriatric rehab, where patients are ambulatory and coachable. |

### 3. Psychographics

Dr. Chen is defined by **professional accountability.** Every recommendation she makes carries her name. She will not recommend a resource that overclaims, misinforms, or generates bad outcomes, because her patients will bring it back to her and ask why. She is a careful reader — she looks at citations, author credentials, and clinical claims before she recommends anything.

She is also, like many clinicians, quietly exhausted by the consumer health landscape. Her patients come in with misinformation from influencers, supplement companies, and social media. She spends real clinical time undoing what her patients have been told. A resource she can trust — that would reduce rather than add to her clinical load — is worth its weight in gold.

### 4. The Moment — Where She Is When She Finds Us

Most likely entry points:

- **A patient brings it up.** A patient mentions the book or the website. She Googles us to see if she can support the recommendation.
- **A peer referral.** A colleague at a conference, in a professional group, or in a continuing-education course mentions us.
- **Her own search.** She is looking for patient education materials on sarcopenia and strength training protocols for older adults. She lands on our content.
- **An article share.** She sees a StrongPath article shared in a professional Slack, Facebook group, or LinkedIn post by a clinician she respects.

**Her emotional state.** Professional. Clinical-eyes-on. Evaluating. She is reading to decide — is this a resource I can recommend without professional risk? She is not emotionally engaged; she is credentialing us in her head while she reads.

### 5. Her Words, Not Ours

Phrases Dr. Chen uses (in professional contexts):

- "patient education on sarcopenia"
- "resistance training protocol for older adults"
- "evidence-based strength training for geriatrics"
- "fall prevention resources"
- "home exercise program for elderly patients"
- "patient-facing sarcopenia education"
- "rehab resources between visits"

She does **not** use — and will bounce from:

- Any "anti-aging" language
- Any "journey" or "transformation" language
- Unsourced clinical claims
- "Hack," "secret," "breakthrough," or "miracle" language

### 6. Where She Lives

**Professional media:**

- *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society*, *Physical Therapy* (APTA's journal), *Journal of Aging and Physical Activity*
- APTA, AOTA, AGS conferences
- Clinician LinkedIn, professional Facebook groups, closed Slack groups for specialty interest areas

**Consumer-facing media she cross-checks:**

- Harvard Health Publishing (her benchmark for patient education)
- NIA (National Institute on Aging)
- Mayo Clinic patient-facing content

**Who she trusts:**

- Peer clinicians with visible credentials and track records
- Research authors she has read in journals
- Evidence-based practice guidelines (APTA, AGS)

### 7. Objections — Why She Doesn't Recommend on First Visit

1. **"Who wrote this? Are they credentialed?"** She scans for author credentials, reviewer credentials, and institutional affiliations within the first page. Anonymous or weakly credentialed content is a non-starter.
2. **"Are the claims defensible?"** She is looking for in-line citations to primary research, proper hedging ("may help," "research suggests"), and no overclaims. The book/product firewall in BRAND.md section 9 is exactly the discipline she is checking for.
3. **"Is this safe for my patient population?"** Her patients often have multiple comorbidities, polypharmacy, and functional limitations. She needs to see that the content understands contraindications and starting points for deconditioned populations.
4. **"Is there an FTC-compliant disclosure?"** If she sees affiliate links or product sales without clear disclosure, it tells her the operation is not professionally run.
5. **"Will my patients think I'm recommending a commercial product?"** Even if the product is good, she does not want to look like she is referring patients to a sales funnel. The editorial register of the site matters to her reputation.

### 8. What Earns Her Trust

1. **Citation discipline, visible.** Dr. Chen's primary trust signal is what she sees in the article body — named peer-reviewed studies cited with author, year, and journal; current institutional sources (Harvard Health Publishing, Mayo Clinic, NIH, ACSM); landmark older studies cited where they remain the standard reference. She recognizes the names — Fielding, Fiatarone, Phillips, Peterson, Paddon-Jones — because they are the actual literature on her caseload's substrate. A platform that cites these rigorously, on every substantive claim, earns her read in a way that no single credential can. The brand-level discipline of citing well over time is the trust signal; per BRAND.md §5, this is also what builds the brand's accumulating authority on the topic.
2. **The book as bestseller credibility.** *Choosing the StrongPath: Reversing the Downward Spiral of Aging* is an Amazon bestseller in Aging, Weight Training, Exercise, and Longevity. The bestseller fact is what makes the book a credibility asset for the brand — market validation that the topic is serious and the framework was listened to. The book sits behind the platform as category proof; Dr. Chen does not need to read it to trust the platform, and she will not read a 2018 book to evaluate a 2026 resource. The book's role for her is positional, not informational.
3. **The platform as the second edition.** Per BRAND.md §5, StrongPath is the second edition of the work *Choosing the StrongPath* started — same intellectual project, current medium, current research. For Dr. Chen, this framing answers the question her reader's eye is already asking: why a website and not an updated book? The answer is that 2026's research-translation work belongs in a website, soon an app, soon AI-enabled — formats the book could not be. She is not reading "the book in HTML"; she is reading the continuation of the work the book started.
4. **An editorial register that signals non-commercial.** Harvard Health Publishing style, not supplement-brand style. Generous whitespace, serif headlines, citation discipline visible.
5. **Honest limits.** "We are not a replacement for physical therapy. If you have recently had surgery, a fall, or a new diagnosis, work with your PT or physician." That sentence earns clinical trust in a way nothing else does.
6. **A clinician-facing page that speaks to her.** Not marketing copy. A page that says "here is what the resource contains, here is the credentialing, here is how to integrate it into patient education, here is what we do not do."

### 9. Messages That Land

**Opener that works for her** (per BRAND.md 4.3):

> "StrongPath is a patient-facing resource in the category defined by *Choosing the StrongPath: Reversing the Downward Spiral of Aging*, an Amazon bestseller in Aging, Weight Training, Exercise, and Longevity. This page is for clinicians evaluating whether to recommend us to patients."

Followed by:

- **What we are:** a patient education platform in the category defined by *Choosing the StrongPath: Reversing the Downward Spiral of Aging*, substantiated by current peer-reviewed research on sarcopenia, resistance training, protein metabolism, and recovery.
- **What we are not:** a clinical service, a replacement for PT, a diagnostic tool, or a medical device.
- **What we cite:** current third-party research — named peer-reviewed studies, Harvard Health Publishing, Mayo Clinic, NIH, academic institutions — with full citations available on every substantive claim. The book is the brand's credibility anchor, not the citation for individual claims.
- **What we do not claim:** we do not claim to treat, cure, or prevent disease. Claims attach to the research literature we cite, not to any StrongPath product.

### 10. Messages That Fail

- Any consumer-conversion urgency applied to her
- Any "join our community" CTA — she is not joining a community, she is evaluating a resource
- Any discount, sale, or promotional framing on the clinician-facing page
- Testimonials as the primary trust signal — she is looking for citations, not testimonials
- Any claim she could be liable for if she repeated it

# Cross-Persona Notes

## Shared Characteristics

All three personas share:

- **Educated.** Four-year degree minimum, many with graduate training.
- **Skeptical.** They have all encountered enough bad health content to have filters.
- **Underserved by their physicians on this specific topic.** Sarcopenia is clinically under-diagnosed. All three know this, whether or not they have the vocabulary for it.
- **Responsive to editorial, not commercial, register.** BRAND.md's Harvard Health / Mayo Clinic visual and tonal direction serves all three.

## Key Distinctions

**Purchase psychology differs fundamentally:**

| | Margaret (Pre-Frail Adult) | David (Proactive Caregiver) | Dr. Chen (Allied Health Pro) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Buying for** | Herself | His parent, and often himself too | Nobody — she recommends, she does not buy |
| **Dominant emotion** | Mild anxiety, refusal of default | Goodwill (*eunoia*); love in action | Professional accountability |
| **Primary ask** | A credible protocol | A credible path for her parent and him; a way to increase good days | A resource she can trust not to damage her reputation |
| **What closes them** | The book + protocol specificity | The book + warm framing of the parent + clinical Q&A + honest safety | Citation discipline + the bestseller credibility behind the platform + the clinician-facing page |
| **Trust velocity** | Medium | Fast in Before moment; fastest in After moment; outside audience in Crisis | Slow — multiple exposures before she recommends |
| **Phase 1 priority** | Primary | Primary (Before and After; do not target Crisis) | Documented; Phase 2 active |

## How to Use These Personas Downstream

**For SEO:** Keyword research should cover Margaret's phrases and David's Before-moment and After-moment phrases as distinct clusters. David's Crisis-moment queries are explicitly out-of-scope. Dr. Chen's search terms are a Phase 2 initiative.

**For Content:** Every article identifies primary persona and (for David) primary moment before the first sentence is drafted. Most Phase 1 articles will serve both Margaret and David, but one is always dominant.

**For Email:** Welcome sequence should branch on quiz responses that signal whether the reader is self-oriented (Margaret) or caregiving (David), and whether David is in the Before or After moment. Tone and pillar emphasis shift accordingly.

**For Paid Media:** Campaign-level separation is required. Margaret creative and David creative should never be the same asset. Within David, Before and After creative should never be the same. Crisis-moment keywords should never be bid on. Dr. Chen is not a paid media target in Phase 1.

**For Community:** Early community tone should welcome Margaret and David as co-primary members. Dr. Chen engagement happens in professional channels, not in our consumer community.

**For any David-facing asset:** Run it against the founder anchor quote before publishing. Does it read as *eunoia*, or does it read as fear? If the answer is fear, rewrite.
