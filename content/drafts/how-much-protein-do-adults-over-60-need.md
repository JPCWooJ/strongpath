# How Much Protein Do Adults Over 60 Need?

Adults over 60 often need more protein than the basic adult minimum. A practical evidence-based range for many healthy older adults is 1.0 to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day.

That range comes from the PROT-AGE Study Group, which recommended that adults over 65 generally aim for at least that amount to help maintain or regain lean body mass and function. [Bauer et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/).

A 70-year-old eating toast and coffee for breakfast may simply not be getting enough protein to support muscle maintenance, especially if lunch and dinner are also light.

Protein can help support muscle maintenance. It does not, by itself, solve age-related muscle loss. The stronger strategy is protein plus progressive resistance training, enough recovery, and medical judgment when needed.

## Why protein matters with aging

Muscle is constantly being broken down and rebuilt. Protein supplies amino acids, the raw materials the body uses for that work.

With age, that process can become less efficient. Illness, inactivity, appetite changes, lower total food intake, and years without strength training can all narrow the margin. The result is not always visible at first. It may show up as a slower walk, a weaker grip, a harder time rising from a chair, or longer recovery after a bad week.

That is why it belongs in the same conversation as sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle strength, muscle quantity, and physical function. If you need the broader definition first, start with [Internal link: What Is Sarcopenia?].

Too little protein makes it harder for the body to maintain the muscle it still has, and harder to respond well when training begins.

## The protein range most adults should know

The standard adult Recommended Dietary Allowance is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. Many geriatric nutrition experts argue that this is a minimum, not an ideal target for many adults over 60.

The PROT-AGE Study Group recommended:

- 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day for many healthy adults over 65
- 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg/day for many older adults with acute or chronic disease
- higher intake only in specific severe illness, injury, or malnutrition contexts, with medical guidance

ESPEN's geriatric nutrition guidance also supports at least 1.0 g/kg/day for many older adults, with higher amounts in illness depending on nutritional status, disease state, and tolerance. [ESPEN guideline, 2019](https://www.espen.org/files/ESPEN-Guidelines/ESPEN_guideline_on_clincal_nutrition_and_hydration_in_geriatrics.pdf).

For a 150-pound adult, 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day is roughly 68 to 82 grams of protein per day.

For a 180-pound adult, it is roughly 82 to 98 grams per day.

Those examples are not targets for everyone. They are a way to make the range concrete.

## Food first, practical options second

This is where the conversation can get noisy.

Protein is a nutrient. It is not a promise, and it is not a shortcut around training, sleep, recovery, or medical care.

Many adults can get meaningful protein from ordinary foods: eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, fish, poultry, lean meat, beans, lentils, tofu, tempeh, milk, or fortified options that fit their preferences and medical needs.

Protein powders can be useful for some people, especially when appetite, chewing, cooking, travel, or convenience make ordinary meals harder. They can also be unnecessary. StrongPath's default posture is food first, practical options second, product claims last.

The first question is not "what powder should I buy?" It is "am I consistently eating enough protein to support the body I want to keep?"

## Protein and resistance training belong together

Protein provides material. Resistance training provides the signal.

If you increase protein but never ask your muscles to work against meaningful resistance, you are missing half the mechanism. If you train consistently but undereat protein, you may not be giving the body enough material to adapt.

That is why this article sits next to the walking and resistance-training articles in the StrongPath cluster. Walking is valuable, but it does not fully train strength. See [Internal link: Why Walking Is Not Enough to Preserve Strength]. The goal is not physique. The goal is simpler: enough muscle and strength to stand, climb, carry, recover, and keep doing the things that make life yours.

## Appetite is part of the problem

Protein advice is easy to write and harder to live.

Many adults eat less with age. Appetite changes. Taste changes. Dental issues, swallowing problems, medications, grief, cooking fatigue, budget, and living alone can all change what ends up on the plate.

This is why vague advice to "eat more protein" often fails. A better plan is more practical:

- notice whether breakfast is mostly toast, cereal, or coffee
- add protein to the meal that is easiest to change
- keep simple options available
- spread protein across the day instead of saving it all for dinner
- make the plan realistic for appetite, budget, chewing, cooking, and routine

Nutrition.gov, which includes resources from USDA and HHS, points older adults toward practical nutrition support for appetite, healthy eating, labels, chewing, swallowing, and other real-world barriers. [Nutrition.gov: Older Adults](https://www.nutrition.gov/topics/nutrition-life-stage/older-adults).

For caregivers, this is often the opening. Do not begin with a lecture. Begin with the meal that is already happening.

## When clinician guidance matters

Protein targets are not one-size-fits-all. Talk with a physician, registered dietitian, or qualified clinician before intentionally increasing protein if you have kidney disease, significant liver disease, complex diabetes management, heart failure, unexplained weight loss, active cancer treatment, swallowing problems, recent hospitalization, or any condition where diet is being medically managed.

Also seek guidance if appetite has dropped suddenly or weight is falling without explanation. That is not a protein math problem. It is a medical signal.

For generally healthy adults, the useful next step is usually simple: compare current intake with the evidence-based range, then decide what change is realistic. Not dramatic. Realistic.

## The StrongPath position

Protein matters after 60 because muscle matters after 60.

The serious pattern is simple: enough protein, progressive resistance training, recovery, and medical judgment when the situation calls for it. Food can carry much of that work. Practical products may help some people execute it. The claim stays the same either way: protein supports the plan; it is not the plan.

## Medical note

This article is educational and is not medical advice. If you have kidney disease, significant liver disease, unexplained weight loss, recent hospitalization, swallowing problems, or a medically managed diet, talk with a physician, registered dietitian, or qualified clinician before changing protein intake.
